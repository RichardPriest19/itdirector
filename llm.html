LLM

Large Language Models – AI systems trained on vast text to understand and generate human-like language

Overview

A Large Language Model (LLM) is an AI system trained on enormous amounts of text to predict and generate language. By learning statistical patterns across billions of examples, an LLM can answer questions, summarise, translate, write and reason in remarkably fluent ways. LLMs are the engine behind today's generative AI tools, and have moved from research curiosity to mainstream business technology in a very short time. Understanding what they can and cannot do is now core to any IT leadership role.

How They Work

Modern LLMs are built on the transformer architecture, which uses a mechanism called attention to weigh the relationships between words in context. Text is broken into tokens (roughly, word fragments), and the model predicts the next token over and over to produce a response. They are first pre-trained on broad data in a self-supervised way, then typically refined through instruction tuning and human feedback so they follow instructions helpfully. The amount of text a model can consider at once is its context window.

Capabilities & Limitations

Strengths Limitations
Fluent language understanding and generation Can "hallucinate" — state false things confidently
Broad general knowledge and reasoning Knowledge is frozen at a training cutoff
Adaptable to many tasks No inherent access to private or live data
Fast to prototype with Cost, latency and limited context windows

Open vs Proprietary

LLMs come in two broad flavours. Proprietary models are consumed as a service via an API — fast to adopt, always maintained, but with usage costs and reliance on a provider. Open-weight models can be downloaded and self-hosted — offering control and data residency, at the cost of needing infrastructure and skills to run them. Many organisations use both, matching the model to the sensitivity, scale and economics of each use case.

IT Director Considerations

  • Build vs buy: API-based proprietary models versus self-hosted open models
  • Data privacy — what leaves the organisation when prompts are sent to a third-party API
  • Accuracy and hallucination risk, especially for customer-facing or high-stakes use
  • Cost and latency at scale, which differ sharply from traditional software
  • Governance and responsible-AI controls (links to ISO 42001 and the NIST AI RMF)
  • How to adapt a model to your needs — prompting, retrieval (RAG) or fine-tuning

Working With LLMs

An out-of-the-box LLM rarely fits a specific business need perfectly. There are three main ways to adapt one, in increasing order of effort: prompt engineering (carefully crafting the input), RAG (retrieval-augmented generation, which supplies the model with your own relevant data at query time), and fine-tuning (further training the model on your data to change its behaviour). These are complementary, and most real deployments combine them — with MLOps practices to run them reliably in production.
