MPLS

Multiprotocol Label Switching – label-based forwarding for fast, predictable, SLA-backed wide-area networking

Overview

MPLS (Multiprotocol Label Switching) is a forwarding technique that moves packets through a network based on short, fixed-length labels rather than performing a full IP address lookup at every hop. Because it sits between the data-link and network layers, it is often described as a "Layer 2.5" technology. For most of the last two decades MPLS was the default choice for enterprise wide-area networks, prized for its predictable performance, quality-of-service guarantees, and provider-backed service-level agreements (SLAs) connecting branch sites, data centres and headquarters.

How It Works

The core idea is "label once, switch many times":

  • At the ingress edge, the provider router classifies a packet and pushes a label onto it.
  • In the core, routers forward purely by swapping labels — a simple, fast operation that avoids repeated IP route lookups.
  • At the egress edge, the label is removed and the packet returns to normal IP forwarding.

The path a labelled packet follows is a Label Switched Path (LSP). Labels are distributed between routers using protocols such as LDP (Label Distribution Protocol) or RSVP-TE. Multiple labels can be stacked — for example an outer transport label plus an inner label identifying a specific customer VPN.

Key Components

Term Role
CE (Customer Edge) Router at the customer site connecting into the provider
PE (Provider Edge) Provider router that adds/removes labels and holds VPN routing
P (Provider core) Core router that switches labels without seeing customer routes
LSP The end-to-end label switched path a packet follows
FEC Forwarding Equivalence Class – packets treated the same way

MPLS VPNs

The most common enterprise use of MPLS is the provider-managed VPN that privately links an organisation's sites:

  • L3VPN — the provider routes between sites using MP-BGP and per-customer VRFs (Virtual Routing and Forwarding tables), keeping each customer's traffic logically separate.
  • L2VPN — the provider carries Layer 2 connectivity (pseudowires, or VPLS for multipoint), making sites appear to share the same LAN segment.

Traffic is kept private through separation, not encryption — an important distinction from internet VPNs.

Traffic Engineering & QoS

MPLS Traffic Engineering (using RSVP-TE) lets providers steer flows along specific paths and reserve bandwidth, rather than simply following the shortest route. Combined with class-of-service marking, this is what allows MPLS to deliver consistent latency and jitter for real-time traffic such as voice and video — historically its biggest selling point over the public internet. Segment Routing (SR-MPLS) is the modern evolution, achieving similar steering with a much simpler control plane.

IT Director Considerations

  • Cost: MPLS circuits are significantly more expensive per megabit than internet broadband
  • Provisioning lead times of weeks or months for new sites, versus days for internet links
  • Provider lock-in and long contract terms; understand exit and migration paths
  • The performance and SLA guarantees that may still justify MPLS for latency-sensitive workloads
  • Hybrid WAN strategy — running MPLS for critical traffic alongside internet for the rest
  • Whether SD-WAN or SASE over internet links can now meet requirements at lower cost

MPLS vs SD-WAN

Use MPLS where you need contractually guaranteed performance for latency-sensitive traffic and are willing to pay a premium for it. Increasingly, organisations are shifting to SD-WAN, which uses ordinary internet connections with intelligent, application-aware path selection — far cheaper and faster to deploy, though performance depends on the underlying internet rather than a provider SLA. Many enterprises now run a hybrid: MPLS for a small set of critical flows and SD-WAN over internet for everything else, often as a stepping stone toward a full SASE architecture.
