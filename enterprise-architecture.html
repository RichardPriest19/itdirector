Enterprise Architecture

Aligning business strategy, processes, information and technology into a coherent whole

Overview

Enterprise Architecture (EA) is the discipline of designing and aligning an organisation's business processes, information, applications and technology so they work together to support its strategy. It is the bridge between what the business is trying to achieve and how IT is structured to deliver it. Done well, EA reduces duplication and complexity, makes change less risky, and ensures technology investment serves business goals rather than accumulating piecemeal. It is a strategic, big-picture function rather than a project-level activity.

The Four Domains

EA is conventionally divided into four interrelated domains, sometimes abbreviated BDAT:

Domain Concern
Business Strategy, processes, capabilities and organisation
Data / Information What information exists and how it is structured and governed
Application The systems and how they interact
Technology Infrastructure, platforms and networks underneath

What EA Produces

  • Current state ("as-is") — an honest picture of how things work today.
  • Future state ("to-be") — the target design that supports the strategy.
  • Roadmap — the sequenced path from current to future state.
  • Principles & standards — the rules that guide consistent decisions.
  • Reference models — reusable patterns and building blocks.

Frameworks

Rather than starting from scratch, most organisations draw on an established framework. The two best known are TOGAF, which provides a step-by-step method for developing architecture, and the Zachman Framework, which provides a classification scheme for organising architectural artefacts. Others include FEAF (US federal government) and DoDAF (defence). Many organisations blend approaches and tailor them heavily rather than adopting one wholesale.

Benefits & Criticisms

The benefits are real: better business–IT alignment, reduced cost and duplication, clearer investment decisions, and more manageable change. But EA has also earned criticism for becoming "ivory tower" — producing elaborate documentation detached from delivery, and slowing teams down. The modern trend is toward lighter, more pragmatic EA that delivers just enough architecture to guide decisions, and that coexists with agile delivery rather than gatekeeping it.

IT Director Considerations

  • Treating EA as a strategic capability that informs investment, not just a documentation exercise
  • Keeping it lightweight and outcome-focused to avoid bureaucracy and stay credible with delivery teams
  • Linking architecture to portfolio and investment decisions so it actually shapes spend
  • Governance that guides without becoming a bottleneck
  • The skills and seniority an effective enterprise architect requires
  • Choosing and tailoring a framework rather than adopting one rigidly

TOGAF vs Zachman

The two dominant frameworks answer different questions. TOGAF is a method — it tells you how to develop and govern an architecture, through a defined, repeatable process. Zachman is a taxonomy — it tells you what to document, by classifying every architectural artefact against a grid of perspectives and questions. They are complementary rather than competing: many organisations use TOGAF's process to drive the work and Zachman's structure to organise the outputs.
