Zachman Framework

A taxonomy for classifying and organising enterprise architecture artefacts

Overview

The Zachman Framework is one of the earliest and most influential enterprise architecture frameworks, devised by John Zachman in the 1980s. Crucially, it is a taxonomy — a way of classifying and organising the artefacts that describe an enterprise — and not a methodology. It does not tell you how to build an architecture or in what order; instead it provides a structured grid that ensures every important perspective and question has been considered. Think of it as a comprehensive checklist or filing system for architecture, rather than a process.

The Matrix

The framework is a two-dimensional grid. The columns are six fundamental questions; the rows are six stakeholder perspectives, moving from the most abstract to the most concrete. Each cell where a row meets a column represents a specific kind of artefact.

Column (question) Describes
What Data and things
How Processes and functions
Where Locations and networks
Who People and roles
When Timing and events
Why Motivation and goals

The rows run from the most abstract to the most concrete perspective — typically Executive (scope), Business Management, Architect, Engineer, Technician, and finally the functioning enterprise itself. Reading down a column shows how one concern (say, data) is expressed at every level from strategy to implementation.

Strengths & Limitations

  • Completeness — the grid makes gaps visible, ensuring no perspective or question is overlooked.
  • Clarity of structure — a precise, logical way to classify and locate artefacts.
  • No method — it deliberately offers no process, so it must be paired with one (such as TOGAF) to actually deliver architecture.
  • Can feel academic — filling in all the cells fully can be onerous and is rarely done exhaustively in practice.

IT Director Considerations

  • Most useful as a classification and completeness tool rather than a delivery method
  • Commonly combined with TOGAF — Zachman to organise the "what", TOGAF for the "how"
  • Pragmatism over completeness: use the cells that add value rather than populating all of them
  • Value as a communication device that connects executive intent to technical detail
  • Risk of over-documentation if treated as an end in itself

Zachman vs TOGAF

Zachman answers what — it classifies and organises architectural artefacts across perspectives and questions, ensuring completeness, but provides no process for creating them. TOGAF answers how — it gives a step-by-step method (the ADM) for developing and governing architecture. They are complementary rather than alternatives: many organisations use TOGAF to drive the work and Zachman to structure and sense-check the outputs. Both sit within the wider discipline of enterprise architecture.
