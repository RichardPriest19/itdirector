Fine-Tuning

Adapting a pre-trained model to a specific task, domain or style through further training

Overview

Fine-tuning is the process of taking a model that has already been trained on broad data and training it further on a smaller, curated dataset to specialise it. Rather than building a model from scratch — which is hugely expensive — fine-tuning adapts an existing one to a particular task, domain or style. The result is a model that behaves more consistently the way you want: matching a house tone of voice, producing a specific output format, or performing well on a narrow, specialised task.

How It Works

You provide a dataset of examples — typically inputs paired with the desired outputs — and continue training the model on them so it adjusts toward those patterns. Full fine-tuning updates all of the model's parameters and is resource-intensive. More common today are parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA, which train only a small set of additional parameters, drastically cutting the cost and compute required while achieving much of the benefit.

When to Use It

  • Consistent style or format — enforce a tone, structure or output schema reliably.
  • Specialised tasks — improve performance on a narrow, well-defined job.
  • Domain language — adapt to specialised terminology or conventions.
  • Efficiency — sometimes let a smaller, cheaper model match a larger one on a specific task.

Fine-tuning is generally not the right tool for adding factual knowledge that changes over time — that is what RAG is for.

Costs & Risks

  • Data effort — preparing a good-quality, representative dataset is the hardest part.
  • Cost & skills — needs ML expertise and compute, though far less than training from scratch.
  • Staleness — a fine-tuned model is a snapshot; new information means re-tuning.
  • Overfitting — too narrow a dataset can degrade general performance.
  • Maintenance — fine-tuned models become assets you must host, version and update.

IT Director Considerations

  • Whether simpler approaches — prompt engineering or RAG — would meet the need first
  • The investment in data preparation, which usually dominates the effort
  • Hosting and lifecycle management of fine-tuned models (an MLOps concern)
  • Data governance and IP around the training data used
  • Re-tuning cadence as requirements and source data evolve
  • Combining fine-tuning (for behaviour) with RAG (for knowledge) where both are needed

Fine-Tuning, RAG & Prompt Engineering

These are the three ways to adapt an LLM, in increasing order of effort. Prompt engineering steers the model through its input alone — cheapest and quickest. RAG supplies the model with external knowledge at query time — best when the gap is missing information. Fine-tuning changes the model's underlying behaviour — best when the gap is how it responds. The usual advice is to start with prompting, add RAG for knowledge, and reach for fine-tuning only when behaviour itself needs to change.
